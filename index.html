Finding seizures in two-second slices of EEG

This project trains EEGNet, a compact convolutional neural network designed for EEG-based classification, to label EEG windows as seizure or non-seizure. This page compares three training experiments using results stored from experiment logs. It does not run inference on EEG signals.

Research only. These are research results, not a clinically validated diagnostic system.

Global view

The setup shared by all three experiments, and what differs between them.

Dataset splits

Validation and test sets are kept unchanged across all three experiments. Only the training set differs. Balancing the training data does not mean validation and test should be balanced too: leaving them alone keeps the evaluation matched to how rare seizures really are.

Interpretation

  • The original model reached a test F1-score of 64.40%.
  • The 1:5 model improved test F1 to 70.47% and recall to 63.39%, and had the highest test precision (79.33%) and PR-AUC (0.7443).
  • The 1:1 model had the highest test F1 (72.73%), recall (71.43%) and ROC-AUC (0.9857).
  • The 1:1 model has lower test precision and PR-AUC than the 1:5 model.
  • This is a trade-off between detecting more seizure windows and raising more false alarms. The best experiment depends on the evaluation objective.
  • 1:1 is best on test F1 here, but it should not automatically be called the best model for every purpose.
Reading the three kinds of score
F1
Combines precision and recall at the one selected threshold.
PR-AUC
Summarizes the precision-recall trade-off across thresholds.
ROC-AUC
Measures ranking discrimination across thresholds.
Accuracy
Can mislead when seizure windows are uncommon: always answering "non-seizure" would score on this test set.

Limitations and cautions

  • Only three patients are in the test set, so generalization claims are limited.
  • Windows overlap by 50% and share samples, so neighboring windows are correlated, not independent.
  • Thresholds were selected on validation data, not on the test set.
  • A high threshold does not mean calibrated probabilities or clinical certainty.
  • Results can vary with random undersampling and training randomness.
  • These are research results, not evidence of clinical safety or a validated diagnostic system.
  • They do not show that 1:1 balancing will always beat 1:5 undersampling or the original data.
